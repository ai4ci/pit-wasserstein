\title{Diagnosing miscalibration in probabilistic estimates with PIT-Wasserstein metrics}
\author{\name Robert Challen, Amber Palmer and Leon Danon\\
\addr AI4CI and Department of Engineering Mathematics, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When a prediction interval misses its target, the estimate may be poorly centred, too narrow,
or both. The Wasserstein distance between the distribution of probability integral transforms
(PITs) and the uniform distribution summarises the discrepancy across a set of predictions. We
derive a large-sample reference for the baseline distance and compare it with two statistics
calculated after estimating a common location shift. One measures the remaining departure
across quantiles. The other gives its direction, with positive values indicating
overconfidence. For independent Gaussian errors of common scale, estimating location changes
the reference distribution of the unsigned statistic but leaves the first-order variance of
the directed statistic unchanged. In simulated epidemics, EpiEstim estimates of the
reproduction number improved on the continuous ranked probability score as case counts
increased. Over the same range, coverage of nominal 95\% intervals fell from 90\% to 51\%, and
the directed statistic changed from negative to positive. Reporting these quantities together
distinguishes a gain in overall accuracy from a deterioration in interval coverage.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
probabilistic calibration, probability integral transform, Wasserstein distance, forecast
evaluation, reproduction number
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

Estimates of an epidemic reproduction number are often reported with an interval. As case
counts increase, narrower intervals may be warranted, but their width alone tells us little
about whether they cover the true value. Sharpness is a property of the predictive
distribution, measured for example by its interquartile range. Calibration concerns the
agreement between the predictive probabilities and the frequencies observed over repeated
predictions \citep{degroot1983,gneiting2007}. We consider the problem of deciding how much of
a discrepancy persists after accounting for a systematic error in the predictive median, and
whether the remaining intervals tend to be too wide or too narrow.

Suppose we have a continuous predictive distribution $F_i$ and an observed or independently
specified reference value $x_i$ for each of $n$ predictions. The probability integral
transform $u_i=F_i(x_i)$ locates the reference value within its prediction. Probabilistic
calibration requires uniform PITs in the population of predictions being evaluated. For the
tests in this paper we additionally require independence across cases
\citep{dawid1984,diebold1998}. A PIT histogram with excess observations near zero and one is
consistent with intervals that are too narrow. Different errors can nevertheless produce
similar histograms, and errors within subgroups can disappear on pooling \citep{hamill2001}.
Coverage at a single interval level also leaves discrepancies at other levels unmeasured.

Wasserstein distance provides a measure of the discrepancy across all quantiles
\citep{panaretos2019}. \citet{zhao2020} used the distance from uniformity to study individual
calibration, and \citet{wessel2025} used a sample version as a penalty when training a
forecasting model. Calibration errors based on differences between nominal and empirical
quantiles have also been used to assess regression models \citep{kuleshov2018,dheur2023}. We
use the existing unadjusted distance to assess the original forecasts. We give it a sampling
reference, estimate a common shift on the scale on which location errors are expected to add,
and derive references for the magnitude and direction of the remaining discrepancy. We examine
what the three statistics distinguish in simulations with known errors, then apply them to
estimates of the reproduction number in epidemics for which the target is known.

\section{Measuring departures from PIT uniformity}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsection{The original and location-adjusted predictions}

Let $m_i=F_i^{-1}(1/2)$ be the predictive median. Choose an increasing link $g$ such that a
common bias is reasonably expressed as an additive error on that scale. The identity link
removes an additive shift in the outcome. The log link removes a common multiplicative factor
for a positive quantity. We estimate the shift once for the set of predictions being evaluated
and evaluate each predictive CDF at its adjusted reference value.
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\delta=\med_i\{g(m_i)-g(x_i)\},\qquad
 x_i^{\rm adj}=g^{-1}\{g(x_i)+\widehat\delta\},\qquad
 v_i=F_i(x_i^{\rm adj}).
 \label{eq:adjust}
\end{equation}
A positive shift means that the predictive medians tended to exceed the reference values on
the chosen scale. Using the median makes the correction appropriate for a skewed error
distribution when the target is median bias. The correction is descriptive and uses the
observed outcomes. Recalibrating future forecasts would require estimating it on separate
data.

Write $r_i=(i-1/2)/n$ for the midpoint of the $i$th of $n$ equal probability intervals. For
any set of PITs $a$, write $a_{(i)}$ for its $i$th smallest value. Define
\begin{align}
 W_n(a)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n |r_i-a_{(i)}|, &
 \Bpit&= W_n(u), & \Upit&= W_n(v), \label{eq:unsigned}\\
 h(t)&=\sgn(1/2-t), &
 \Dpit&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (r_i-v_{(i)})h(r_i). &&\label{eq:directed}
\end{align}
The raw and adjusted PITs are ranked separately. Adding the same shift to two observations can
change their PIT ordering if the predictions have different scales. In one dimension
Wasserstein-1 distance is the integral of the absolute difference between quantile functions.
Thus $W_n$ is exactly the distance to the discrete uniform measure on the midpoints. Its
difference from the distance to the continuous uniform law is at most $1/(4n)$, the mean
distance from a uniform draw to its closest midpoint. This distinction matters in small
samples. Even independent uniform draws generally have a positive baseline score.

\subsection{Direction and magnitude}

For an overconfident forecast, low adjusted PITs tend to lie below their uniform ranks and
high PITs above theirs. Both contribute positively to equation~\eqref{eq:directed}. For a
conservative forecast both contribute negatively. Opposing departures can cancel in $\Dpit$,
whereas $\Upit$ accumulates their magnitudes. For even $n$, if $P$ and $M$ are the sums of the
positive and absolute negative contributions to $\Dpit$ (including the factor $1/n$), then
$\Dpit=P-M$ and $\Upit=P+M$. At odd $n$ the middle rank has zero signed weight, and its
absolute discrepancy also contributes to $\Upit$. In either case $|\Dpit|\leq\Upit$. The
unsigned scores lie between 0 and $1/2$, and the signed score between $-1/4$ and $1/4$.

In a location family on the link scale, displacing all forecasts by the same amount increases
$\widehat\delta$ by that amount. The adjusted PITs are then unchanged, even if the prediction
scales differ. An alternative implemented in \texttt{pitw} subtracts the sample median of
$\Phi^{-1}(u_i)$ on the probit scale. Here and below $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ are the standard
normal distribution and density. With Gaussian errors of common scale the two adjustments
coincide. With varying scales an outcome shift of $b$ corresponds to a different standardised
shift $b/s_i$ for each prediction. A single correction estimated from the PITs alone therefore
answers a different question.

A simple case gives the adjustment a numerical interpretation. Suppose $m_i-x_i$ is normally
distributed with mean one and standard deviation one, and each forecast reports standard
deviation one. Every population PIT quantile lies below its uniform counterpart. The
population baseline distance, also the population counterpart of the unadjusted penalty of
\citet{wessel2025}, is $1/2-\mathbb{E}[u_i]=\Phi(1/\sqrt{2})-1/2=0.260$ to three decimal
places. After correcting the unit shift the population values of both adjusted scores are
zero. Conversely, an error in spread alone can produce appreciable values of all three scores.

\section{Sampling references for independent predictions}
\label{sec:null}

\subsection{Baseline distance}

Let $Q_n$ be the empirical quantile function of the raw PITs. Under independent uniform
sampling, $\sqrt n\{t-Q_n(t)\}$ converges to a standard Brownian bridge $G(t)$, with
covariance $K(s,t)=\min(s,t)-st$ \citep{bhattacharya2021}. The bridge describes how far
ordered uniform observations fluctuate about their expected positions. The midpoint
approximation in equation~\eqref{eq:unsigned} vanishes on this scale. Taking the integrated
absolute value gives
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\,\Bpit\ \Longrightarrow\ L_B=\int_0^1|G(t)|\,dt,
 \qquad \mathbb{E}L_B=\sqrt{2/\pi}\int_0^1\!\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}8 .
 \label{eq:baseline-limit}
\end{equation}
Where the Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic measures the largest discrepancy, this statistic
measures the area. \citet{johnson-killeen-1983} give the distribution function of $L_B$. Its
95th and 99th percentiles are 0.5821 and 0.7519, respectively. A one-sided test at the 5\%
level therefore compares $\Bpit$ with $0.5821/\sqrt n$. The series evaluates the law of the
limiting bridge functional. Its use at a given $n$ is an approximation.

\subsection{Estimating location from the evaluation sample}

For the adjusted references we assume that, on the chosen link scale, the predictive
distributions are Gaussian with common standard deviation $s$ and that
$g(x_i)-g(m_i)=-\delta+sZ_i$, with $Z_i$ independent standard normals. The true shift $\delta$
may be non-zero. After equation~\eqref{eq:adjust}, $v_i=\Phi(Z_i-\med_j Z_j)$. Estimating
location from the sample reduces the variability of its median PIT. To see how that affects
other quantiles, write $z_t=\Phi^{-1}(t)$ and expand the adjusted quantile
$\Phi(Z_{(i)}-\med_j Z_j)$ about $z_t$ with $r_i$ near $t$. The first-order change due to
centring is $-\varphi(z_t)\med_j Z_j$. The sample median on the normal scale has the
PIT-quantile fluctuation at $t=1/2$ divided by $\varphi(0)$. Thus the bridge for the adjusted
ranks is
\begin{equation}
 G_A(t)=G(t)-k(t)G(1/2),\qquad
 k(t)=\frac{\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(t))}{\varphi(0)},\qquad
 \sqrt n\,\Upit\ \Longrightarrow\ L_U=\int_0^1 |G_A(t)|\,dt .
 \label{eq:adjusted-limit}
\end{equation}
In particular $G_A(1/2)=0$. Applying the baseline critical value after fitting the shift gives
too few rejections in this model. We evaluate $L_U$ numerically by simulating 20,000 bridges
on a grid of 1,000 points, removing the fitted median component from each and integrating its
absolute value. The estimated 95th percentile is about 0.39, roughly two-thirds of the
baseline value. The package caches the simulations and extrapolates the survival probability
above the simulated 99th percentile. Very small reported tail probabilities therefore depend
on the extrapolation. An explicit distribution function or a high-precision tabulation would
improve this part of the calculation.

\subsection{The directed statistic}

The directed statistic is linear in the quantile discrepancy. In the Gaussian model
$k(t)=k(1-t)$ while $h(t)=-h(1-t)$, so the estimated-location term integrates to zero.
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\,\Dpit\ \Longrightarrow\ \int_0^1 h(t)G_A(t)\,dt
 =\int_0^1 h(t)G(t)\,dt\ \sim\ N(0,1/48).
 \label{eq:directed-limit}
\end{equation}
The limit is Gaussian because it is a linear functional of a Gaussian process. To obtain its
variance, integrate $h(s)h(t)K(s,t)$ over the unit square. The $\min(s,t)$ term gives
$\int_0^1[\int_x^1 h(t)\,dt]^2dx=2\int_0^{1/2}x^2dx=1/12$. The $st$ term gives $[\int_0^1 t
h(t)\,dt]^2=1/16$. Their difference is $1/48$. The resulting two-sided 5\% threshold is
$|\Dpit|>0.2829/\sqrt n$. For example, with 100 independent predictions the three 5\%
thresholds are 0.0582 for $\Bpit$, about 0.039 for $\Upit$ and 0.0283 for $|\Dpit|$.

The baseline reference applies to independent uniform PITs from any continuous predictive
family. Equations~\eqref{eq:adjusted-limit} and~\eqref{eq:directed-limit} describe the effect
of estimating a median in the specified common-scale Gaussian model. The density at each
quantile determines the effect of a shift. Changing the family, varying the scales or
introducing dependence can therefore require a different reference. In particular, a
population directed score of zero alone does not imply variance $1/(48n)$.

\section{Simulation results}
\label{sec:simulation}

\subsection{Bias, reported spread and error spread}

We simulated 1,000 estimators, each making 1,000 Gaussian predictions. For each prediction we
drew a reference $x_i$ uniformly on $[-1,1]$ and an error $e_i\sim N(\mu_{\mathcal
E},\sigma_{\mathcal E}^2)$, and reported $N(x_i+e_i,s_i^2)$. The reported standard deviation
$s_i$ was drawn independently from a lognormal distribution with median $\sigma_{\mathcal S}$
and coefficient of variation $\kappa$. Between estimators we varied the mean location error
uniformly over $[-1,1]$, each scale independently and log-uniformly over $[1/2,2]$, and
$\kappa$ uniformly over $[0,2]$. The ratio $c=\sigma_{\mathcal S}/\sigma_{\mathcal E}$
measures dispersion calibration in this design. At $c=1$, the typical reported spread matches
the error spread. When $\kappa>0$, reported uncertainty also varies from case to case without
any corresponding change in the error distribution. This differs from a correctly specified
forecast whose conditional variance genuinely varies between cases.

At a given spread ratio, larger location errors generally increased both mean continuous
ranked probability score (CRPS) and $\Bpit$ (Figure~\ref{fig:metrics}A,B). CRPS integrates the
squared difference between each predictive CDF and the observation step function, in outcome
units. Lower values indicate better overall predictive performance. The PIT statistics instead
compare quantiles over the set of predictions. After adjustment, $\Upit$ was lowest close to
$c=1$ and increased as reported spread varied more independently of the errors (panel C). The
directed statistic crossed zero close to $c=1$ and distinguished narrow from wide forecasts
across the range of $\kappa$ studied (panel D).

There is an exact population symmetry in the constant-scale Gaussian case. After removing
location bias, the PIT CDF is $H_c(t)=\Phi(c\Phi^{-1}(t))$. Since $H_{1/c}=H_c^{-1}$,
reflection in the diagonal preserves the area between the CDF and the uniform reference.
Forecasts with twice and half the correct standard deviation therefore have the same
population adjusted distance and opposite signed discrepancies. Variation in reported spread
mixes these curves. At a typical spread ratio of one it can produce excess mass both near the
PIT endpoints and near the centre, leaving a small net direction despite a measurable unsigned
departure. The \vignette{introduction-to-pit}{PIT histogram examples} show the individual
predictions behind these patterns. The \vignette{pit-w-metrics}{metric comparison} includes
coverage of the central 50\% interval.

\subsection{Sample size and rejection rates}

We first compared 100 replicate estimators per combination of spread ratio ($2/3$, 1 or
$3/2$), mean location error (0, 0.5 or 1 outcome units) and sample size (10, 100 or 1,000).
The error standard deviation varied between estimators, while reported spread was constant
within each estimator. At ten predictions there was considerable overlap between groups
(Figure~\ref{fig:limits}). At 100 predictions the directed score more clearly separated the
conservative and overconfident groups. The baseline score remained sensitive to location error
at each spread ratio.

Small samples also affected the false-positive rate. In a separate check with 4,000 replicates
per sample size and correctly specified common-scale Gaussian forecasts, the baseline,
directed and adjusted unsigned tests rejected in 4.6\%, 7.1\% and 1.8\% of replicates at
$n=10$. At $n=100$ the rates were 5.0\%, 5.1\% and 4.7\%. The departures at ten observations
were in different directions for the two adjusted statistics. The
\vignette{sample-sizes-and-power}{sample-size study} gives the design behind
Figures~\ref{fig:limits} and~\ref{fig:curves}.

\subsection{Predictive family and interpretation of the three scores}

For a positive quantity we also compared gamma and lognormal generating families with gamma,
lognormal and normal reported distributions. For each generating family we drew a
multiplicative error $w_i$ with median one and specified coefficient of variation, set the
predictive median to $1/w_i$, and evaluated the forecast at a reference value of one. The
reported distribution had the same median and coefficient of variation across candidate
families. We used 2,000 predictions per estimator, 20 replicates per combination and
coefficients of variation from 0.01 to 0.8. Reporting a normal distribution for a skewed
quantity produced a growing $\Upit$ while $\Dpit$ remained close to zero. Exchanging gamma and
lognormal families changed the sign of $\Dpit$ and could resemble a width error. Normal
predictions for a positive quantity can also put mass outside its support. The
\vignette{model-misspecification}{distributional misspecification study} examines these
comparisons and the effect of a multiplicative location bias.

For an estimator of unknown quality we report $\Bpit$ for the original predictions, the
estimated location shift, then $\Dpit$ and $\Upit$ for the adjusted predictions. A large
baseline distance that diminishes after centring is consistent with a common location error. A
large directed score describes the net direction of interval error. A large unsigned score
alongside a small directed score directs attention to departures that cancel, for example
different errors in the two tails or across subgroups. The same sign can arise from more than
one mechanism, as the family comparisons illustrate. All three tests use the same
observations. Their marginal $p$-values describe the evidence for each comparison and provide
no joint 5\% classification rule. A small baseline score can coexist with conditional errors,
so it is also informative to examine the adjusted scores and relevant subgroups in their own
right.

\section{Estimating the reproduction number in simulated epidemics}
\label{sec:epi}

We applied EpiEstim \citep{cori2013} to simulated case counts so that each estimate of the
instantaneous reproduction number could be compared with a known trajectory. In a
deterministic susceptible--infectious--recovered model, the true value on day $t$ was $R_t=R_0
S_t$, where $S_t$ is the susceptible proportion. We set $R_0=2$, used a mean infectious period
of five days and started with an infectious proportion of $10^{-4}$. We followed the same
epidemic curve for 100 days in each of 100 populations, spaced logarithmically from about 316
to 100,000 people. At each size we generated ten independent series of observed counts by
binomial sampling with probability 0.5 from the rounded daily model incidence. Increasing
population size therefore increased the case counts available for estimation while preserving
epidemic shape and ascertainment.

EpiEstim received the observed counts and a parametric serial interval with mean and standard
deviation both five days. We specified a gamma prior on $R$ with mean two and standard
deviation two. We fitted overlapping windows of eight daily counts, advancing the start by one
day at each fit. For each retained estimate we took the reported posterior mean and standard
deviation to define a gamma distribution, and followed the day matching in the worked example.
The incidence vector begins at simulation day zero, whereas EpiEstim numbers its entries from
one. Joining the numerical window-end index to the simulation day therefore scores the
estimate against the following day. We retained reference days with a positive observed count.
A median of 76 of the 87 candidate estimates contributed to each epidemic. We used a log link
in equation~\eqref{eq:adjust}. The outcome-scale bias displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:epi}A is
the mean of $x_i-m_i$, so a negative value here means that EpiEstim tended to overestimate
$R_t$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
 \centering\includegraphics[width=.89\linewidth]{fig/fig4-epiestim-by-epi-size.pdf}
 \caption{EpiEstim estimates scored against the simulated reproduction number. Each point is
 one epidemic and the horizontal axis gives peak observed daily cases among scored days.
 Panels show mean reference minus predictive median (A), baseline distance (B), directed
 statistic (C) and adjusted unsigned distance (D). Black lines are local regression smooths.
 In B--D, red indicates rejection at the nominal 5\% level under independence and blue
 indicates non-rejection. Overlapping windows invalidate that significance comparison. We
 interpret the scores and smooths descriptively.}
 \label{fig:epi}
\end{figure}

We grouped the 1,000 epidemics by peak observed count into eight equal-sized groups and
averaged the scenario-level scores within each group. From the lowest to highest group, the
mean peak increased from 10 to 1,297 cases per day. The mean CRPS fell from 0.182 to 0.087 in
units of $R_t$, while coverage of the nominal 95\% intervals fell from 90\% to 51\%. The mean
baseline distance increased from 0.240 to 0.298. The mean directed score changed from $-0.074$
to $+0.089$, with the fitted curve crossing zero near 57 peak cases per day. The adjusted
unsigned curve was lowest near 47 cases (Figure~\ref{fig:epi}C,D). Thus intervals were
relatively wide at low counts and narrow at high counts after correcting the common location
error. Narrow intervals and a declining location error can improve the overall score even as
coverage deteriorates.

These are repeated estimates along a declining $R_t$ trajectory. Adjacent windows share
observations, so counting each estimate as an independent prediction would overstate the
information in the series. The descriptive agreement between coverage and the directed score
supports a change in interval behaviour with case count in this design. The prior, the length
and alignment of the window, and the incidence likelihood could each contribute to this
pattern. We held all of them fixed. The \vignette{sir-epiestim-example}{EpiEstim worked
example} supplies the model and scoring code.

\section{Discussion}

The baseline distance answers a question about the predictive distributions as issued. A
global location adjustment answers a second question that is particularly relevant when
developing an estimator. If its median were consistently shifted on an appropriate scale, how
much of the discrepancy between observed and predicted quantiles would remain? The directed
and unsigned statistics describe complementary aspects of that discrepancy. The adjusted
reference distributions account for the use of the same data to estimate the shift in the
common-scale Gaussian model. Using a common critical value for the two unsigned statistics
would have obscured much of the evidence for independently varying spread in our simulations.

These diagnostics complement a proper score. A proper score assesses overall predictive
performance on a specified outcome scale, and a score decomposition can attribute some of that
performance to calibration \citep{gneiting2023}. Our distances give equal weight across PIT
ranks and use probability units. In the EpiEstim example the overall score improved while the
nominal intervals covered fewer reference values. Reporting interval width, bias and coverage
with the PIT statistics makes that distinction interpretable. The adjusted scores alone would
also discard information about the systematic overestimation of $R_t$.

Three limitations affect their use. First, pooling assumes that a single location shift is a
meaningful summary. Opposite biases by forecast horizon or subgroup can average out in the PIT
distribution and require separate evaluation. Second, the projected-bridge calculation uses a
common Gaussian scale on the link scale. A family-specific projection or a simulation that
reproduces the forecast scales, the fitted shift and any dependence would be needed to justify
comparable tests in other settings. Third, the ordinary PIT assumes continuous univariate
predictions. Discrete outcomes require an appropriate randomised transform
\citep{brockwell2007}, with that extra variation carried through the analysis. Multivariate
outcomes require a choice of joint diagnostic.

For independent predictions from the reference model, the three statistics can be accompanied
by large-sample $p$-values. In settings such as rolling epidemic estimates, the scores retain
a descriptive interpretation even when their nominal thresholds do not apply. The R package
\texttt{pitw} provides \texttt{pit\_w()} for individual sets of predictions and
\texttt{prediction\_scores()} for grouped comparisons with CRPS and coverage. The
\href{https://ai4ci.github.io/pit-wasserstein/articles/}{package articles} give worked
examples, and the \vignette{significance-testing}{significance-testing vignette} gives the
numerical evaluation of the reference distributions.

\section{Numerical evaluation of the tests}

For completeness, write $a'_j$ for the negative zeros of the derivative of the Airy function
$\mathrm{Ai}$, ordered by increasing magnitude, and $d_j=|a'_j|/2^{1/3}$. The series of
\citet{johnson-killeen-1983} for equation~\eqref{eq:baseline-limit} is
\begin{align*}
 \Pr(L_B\leq x)&=\sqrt{\pi/2}\sum_{j\geq1}d_j^{-3/2}
      \psi\big(x/d_j^{3/2}\big),\qquad x>0,\\
 \psi(y)&=3^{2/3}y^{-1/3}\exp\{-2/(27y^2)\}\,
             \mathrm{Ai}\big((3y)^{-4/3}\big).
\end{align*}
The package evaluates this series for the baseline statistic and uses normal tail
probabilities for the directed statistic. The following R code reproduces the
independent-prediction checks in Section~\ref{sec:simulation} with the median adjustment. The
locations and errors are in units of one error standard deviation. Run the code in a fresh R
session so that the random seed also determines the cached adjusted reference.

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
library(pitw)
set.seed(2861)
critical <- c(B = q_l1_bridge(.95), D = qnorm(.975) / sqrt(48),
              U = u_pit_significance(NA_real_, 1)$critical_0.05)

rejections <- function(n, bias, ratio, cv, reps) {
  ranks <- (seq_len(n) - .5) / n
  rejects <- replicate(reps, {
    error <- rnorm(n, bias)
    sd_i <- if (cv == 0) ratio else
      rlnorm(n, log(ratio), sqrt(log1p(cv^2)))
    raw <- sort(pnorm(-error / sd_i))
    adj <- sort(pnorm((median(error) - error) / sd_i))
    score <- c(B = mean(abs(ranks - raw)),
               D = mean((ranks - adj) * sign(.5 - ranks)),
               U = mean(abs(ranks - adj)))
    sqrt(n) * abs(score) > critical
  })
  100 * rowMeans(rejects)
}
sapply(c(10, 25, 100, 400), function(n) rejections(n, 0, 1, 0, 4000))

                   c(0, 1.3, 0), c(0, 1, 1), c(1, 1.3, 0))
